Robotproof

Make something robots can't open. Photograph or describe anything with a lid, a door or a drawer. Aholo Lux3D builds it in 3D and works out how its parts move. You open it with your mouse. Then a robot arm tries, without being told how it opens.

Live: https://robotproof.vercel.app

From the idea to the result

The question. Robots still fumble everyday things people open without thinking, and the 3D objects robots practise on in simulation are built and articulated by hand. Labelling the parts and joints of 5,402 objects for one 2026 dataset took 400 hours of human correction, even with automatic proposals (Artiverse, arXiv 2605.24403). Manycore co-founder Huang Xiaohuang puts the need plainly: "Robots must be trained in virtual environments before they can operate in the real world." (KrASIA, 12 Dec 2025)

The idea. Lux3D's articulation step takes a 3D model and one sentence ("the lid swings up on its back hinge") and returns the object's real kinematics: separate part meshes and a URDF with a hinge or slide joint, its axis and its limits. That turns any generated object into something that can be opened, by a person and by a robot, and loaded into a robot simulator.

The game. You go first, with the mouse, in real physics. Then the robot gets three tries, seeing only the shape of each part. If you can open it and the robot can't, it's robot-proof, and it goes on the Robot Gym wall for others to try and download.

How it's built

1. Make: a sentence goes to Lux3D text-to-3D, or a photo goes to Aholo's asset storage and then to Lux3D image-to-3D (G1-Turbo, 60,000 faces). 2. Joints: the model and the "what should open" sentence go to Lux3D articulation, which returns a zip with robot.urdf and one GLB per part. 3. Pack: our server compresses the parts and stores them (Lux3D's links expire after 2 hours). 4. Play: the browser turns each URDF link into a rigid body and each joint into a Rapier hinge or slide joint with the same limits. 5. Export: the download has the URDF with estimated masses and inertias, OBJ meshes for simulators and the textured GLBs, and it loads and moves in MuJoCo 3.3.7 (checked on every build by ./check.sh).

The robot

The robot is not given the joint. It sees each moving part's shape and where it sits, picks the point that sticks out most (a knob, a lid's lip), and tries up to three moves in the order the shape suggests: lift, pull, sweep left, sweep right, push down. Its grip is a held point capped at 60 N, it slips if the part won't follow, and a try counts if the part ends more than half open. Before every try the object's physics is rebuilt at rest. It is a scripted set of moves on a drawn two-link arm, not a learned policy, and we say so in the app.

Teaching it: one demonstration

While you drag a part, the page records where you grabbed it, in the part's own frame, and where that point went, at physics rate. After a STUMPED, the robot grips the same spot and follows that path, moving on only while the part keeps up, with the same force cap and slip rule. It never reads the joint. The drag also ships in the download as demonstrations.json: grip point, positions and open fraction over time, in the URDF's frame.

Claim: shown once, the robot opens 15 of 19 openable objects (79%, 95% interval 57–91%), against 4 without help (21%) and 6 with the same hand and no demonstration (32%).
Evidence type: our own break test, not real users. The demonstration is a scripted stand-in for a person's drag: the pull along each joint, recorded as the path of the part's centre. What doesn't work yet

A new object takes about 5 to 10 minutes: Lux3D builds the model (about 1.5 to 5 minutes), then finds the joints (about 4 to 5 minutes). Joint ranges can be off, as with the pencil box. Masses are estimates (surface area × 12 mm wall × 600 kg/m³), not measurements. The robot is a scripted grasp, not a trained controller. To keep credits for everyone, each visitor can make 3 objects a day and the site at most 60 a day.
